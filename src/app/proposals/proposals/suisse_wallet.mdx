## Heavy on capabilities, light on intent


### Comprehensive set of capabilities
Suisse Wallet has shipped an ambitious range of features — from atomic swaps and 
Lightning payments to Ghost Mode, P2P trading, and a dead man's switch for inheritance. 
The technical ambition is real, and some of these capabilities are genuinely novel in the wallet space.
But this is where product intent gets blurred.
Each feature serves a different user with a different mental model 
and a different reason to care:

- **Ghost Mode** → privacy-first users fleeing surveillance
- **Swaps** → DeFi traders optimizing for trustlessness
- **Pay** → everyday users who just want to pay for coffee
- **P2P swaps** → OTC traders moving large amounts discreetly
- **Inherit** → long-term holders planning for the worst
- **Sovereignty score** → crypto idealists tracking their self-custody progress

Each of these is a different person. Right now, Suisse Wallet is trying to be all of them.
The question isn't what to build next. It's who to build for.

## Orchestration starts with a target audience

Suisse Wallet's brand already makes the choice — it just hasn't committed to it yet.

Ghost Mode, no-KYC hardware wallet, XMR atomic swaps, the Ledger breach angle: every bold move points at the same person. 
Not the DeFi trader chasing yield. Not the long-term holder checking prices once a week. The privacy-first user — 
someone who believes financial privacy is a right, not a feature, and who makes product choices accordingly.

This audience is underserved and underestimated. Privacy-first users are often dismissed as a niche, but 
they have three characteristics that make them disproportionately valuable:

- **They stay.** Once they find a platform they trust, they don't leave. Switching costs are high — 
not because of lock-in, but because trust is hard to rebuild.
- **They refer.** Privacy communities are tight. A recommendation from a trusted member carries more weight 
than any paid acquisition channel.
- **They tolerate friction.** They will go through more steps, learn more concepts, and accept more 
complexity than the average user — as long as they believe in the mission.

Today, no wallet puts privacy first and executes on it comprehensively. Cake Wallet does well for Monero. 
Wasabi does well for Bitcoin coinjoin. But no one owns the full stack: pay, swap, inherit, invoice — all privacy-preserving, all non-custodial, all in one place. That's a blue ocean.

The lifetime value of a Suisse Wallet user who converts could be significant — not because of transaction fees alone, 
but because this audience manages meaningful amounts, transacts regularly, and doesn't churn.

Traders, long-term holders, and crypto-curious newcomers are interesting secondary markets. 
But trying to serve them at the same time dilutes everything. 
Pick the privacy-first user. Build for them obsessively. The others will follow.

## Structure the features for privacy-first users
Choosing an audience is only half the work. The other half is translating that choice into a product 
that feels inevitable — where every feature reinforces the same intent.

I see two self-reinforcing intents Suisse Wallet can build around:

- **Privacy for everyday life.** Not privacy as a panic button, but privacy as a default — 
embedded in payments, transfers, and savings.
- **Visible progress.** Users should feel themselves becoming more financially sovereign over time. 
Not through education, but through use.

These two intents create a compounding loop: people use the wallet because it provides immediate value, 
and the wallet reflects back how far they've come. Nothing drives retention like feeling more competent in something 
you deeply care about.

This framing has a direct consequence for the product: not all features are equal. Some earn prime real estate. 
Others serve advanced users who will find them regardless.

### Prime features

Four features carry the core experience:

- **Pay.** Lightning-powered, QR-native, fast. This is the daily touchpoint — 
the reason the wallet lives on your phone and not in a drawer.
- **Transfer.** Reframe "Lightning" as a use case, not a protocol. 
Remittances, savings, investments — private, instant, borderless. The protocol is invisible. The outcome isn't.
- **Sovereignty Score.** The feedback loop made visible. How private am I today? What's my next step? 
This turns a static tool into a living practice — and gives Suisse Wallet a retention mechanic no competitor has.
- **Key Split.** Allow users to distribute their private key across trusted contacts — by email or phone number. 
Elegant solution to the single-point-of-failure problem every self-custody user dreads. Also a natural referral mechanic: 
to use it, you bring someone else in.

Together these four features address the core jobs-to-be-done of a privacy-first user — 
transact, transfer, track progress, stay safe — while creating a usage cycle that turns an app into a daily companion.

### Secondary features

Ghost Mode, Inherit, and Invoicing remain valuable — but for a narrower slice of the audience. 
They don't need to disappear, they need to step back. Accessible to the expert user who seeks them out, 
but not competing for attention with the core experience. In practice: a secondary navigation layer, 
discoverable but not advertised.

This isn't about cutting capabilities. It's about respecting the user's attention.

### A note on trading

Swaps and P2P trading serve a meaningfully different user with a meaningfully different mental model. 
The DeFi trader optimizing for slippage and the privacy-first user paying for coffee are not the same person — 
and designing for both simultaneously produces an experience that serves neither well.

The trading capabilities are real and valuable. But they belong in a focused product: **Private Trade** — 
a dedicated interface built around Trading View's industry-standard UX, augmented with Suisse Wallet's atomic swap and 
P2P infrastructure. Same engine, different shell. The privacy-first user who occasionally trades can access it. 
The serious trader gets a tool built for them.

This isn't splitting the company. It's giving each user what they actually came for.

## Distribution: own the channel, own the trust

For a privacy-first audience, distribution is not a marketing problem. It's a trust problem. Every touchpoint — how they find you, how they download you, how they update you — is a signal about who you are.
With that lens, three channels matter:

1- **Own your webapp**

Own the infrastructure, own the experience. No app store intermediary, 
no review process that could expose your roadmap, no platform risk. For a privacy-first audience, 
a web app you control is more trustworthy than one gated by a corporation — as long as you're transparent 
about your stack and security practices. This is also your lowest-friction entry point for new users who want to explore 
before committing to a download.

2- **iOS**

Apple's privacy track record is imperfect but credible enough for this audience — 
and iOS dominates among the tech-forward, privacy-conscious demographic Suisse Wallet is targeting. 
An iOS app is not a compromise. It's a reach decision. The wallet needs to live in people's pockets to 
become a daily companion. There is no version of "privacy for everyday life" that doesn't have a mobile app.

3- **Strategic partnerships**

The most underrated channel for this audience. Privacy-first users already cluster around a handful of trusted platforms — 
Proton, Mullvad, Signal, Tutanota. A white-label integration or a "powered by Suisse Wallet" arrangement with 
any one of these puts you in front of your exact target user, with a warm introduction from a brand they already trust. 
No paid acquisition can replicate that.

**One deliberate omission: desktop.** A desktop-first wallet optimized for trading is a different product for a different user. 
The privacy companion lives on mobile. Investment in desktop distribution — beyond basic maintenance — 
pulls in the wrong direction.
The hardware wallet is its own distribution channel and deserves separate treatment. It's not just a product — 
it's a physical object in someone's home, a daily reminder of who Suisse Wallet is. Done right, it's the most powerful trust-building tool in the lineup.


## Conclusion: win on focus, not breadth
The wallet space is crowded. Everyone has swaps. Everyone has staking. Everyone has a roadmap that promises 
more features next quarter. Competing on breadth is a race to the bottom — and a race Suisse Wallet cannot win against 
teams with more funding and more engineers.

But competing on focus? That's a different game entirely.
No wallet owns the privacy-first user. Not comprehensively. Not with the full stack — pay, transfer, inherit, invoice, hardware — all non-custodial, all privacy-preserving, all designed 
for someone who treats financial sovereignty as a principle, not a preference. That space is open. 
And Suisse Wallet, with the capabilities it has already built, is closer to owning it than any competitor.
The work ahead is not building more. It's making deliberate choices about what matters, to whom, and in what order. 
Orchestrating what exists into something that feels inevitable to the right user.

Every product I've shipped that worked had one thing in common: a clear answer to "who is this for?" Hestia, a rental dApp built on EAS attestations, worked because it was built for one user — a French renter navigating an opaque market — not for renters in general. The Unlock Protocol integration worked because it solved one specific problem, with full ownership, end to end. Focus wasn't a constraint. It was the strategy.
Suisse Wallet has the ingredients, the technical depth, and a hardware wallet bet that could change the conversation entirely. 
What it needs now is a recipe — and the discipline to follow it.

Build the katana, not the Swiss Army knife.
